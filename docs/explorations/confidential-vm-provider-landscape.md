# Few Rentals Match Google's Attestable TDX Baseline

The research notes this report draws on ("the notes" below) are kept on the private pod PoC's Linear issue, FIN-494, as an attached archive with an index document.

As of November 2026, confidential VMs of the reader's shape (8 vCPU, 32 GB, no GPU) can be rented from all six big clouds, although IBM's offer is barely documented and Alibaba's TDX runs only in China, Hong Kong and Singapore. They can also be had as managed containers on Tinfoil and Phala Cloud, or built on rented AMD or Intel bare metal. Only Intel TDX with a hypervisor that stays out of the guest gives what the reader proved on Google Cloud C3: a hardware-signed quote whose registers recompute offline from the customer's own unified kernel image (UKI). That leaves Google's own TDX families (C3, and C4 since 23 September 2026), Alibaba's g8i TDX (thinly documented), and TDX on bare metal the customer runs. Azure's TDX and SEV-SNP VMs put a Microsoft paravisor inside the trust boundary. It is open source but its builds are not reproducible and it has no published reference values, so the customer's UKI is pinned only through a vTPM that the paravisor runs. AWS offers only SEV-SNP on 2021-era Milan chips. There the AMD-signed measurement covers nothing past AWS's firmware, and the kernel can be pinned only through NitroTPM documents that AWS signs. Google's SEV-SNP on N2D has the same gap. Tinfoil and Phala/dstack measure their own reproducible images and pin the customer's containers by digest, which is real transparency. But they run on bare metal whose location they either do not document (Tinfoil) or vouch for only by human witnesses (Phala on OVH and OpenMetal). Since TEE.fail (October 2025) and DDRop (September 2026), a DDR5 interposer costing $159 to $1,000 forges attestation on every current TDX and SNP chip. Proof of where a chip sits is therefore part of verification, not an extra, and Google's instance-naming quote plus public host registry remains the strongest such proof found. The recommended hands-on order is GCP C4 TDX first, then Azure DCesv6 TDX, Tinfoil Containers, Phala Cloud, AWS m6a SEV-SNP, OCI E6 SEV-SNP, and finally a rented bare-metal reference build. Most of the two-day tests cost $35 to $60 each. The exceptions are Tinfoil's possible platform fee and bare metal billed by the month.

Google's footprint is the broadest for TDX. C3 runs in 24 zones and C4 in 15, local-SSD C3 variants went GA with TDX on 2026-09-02, and TDX on C4 went to preview on 2026-07-31 and GA on 2026-09-23 ([GCP supported configurations](https://docs.cloud.google.com/confidential-computing/confidential-vm/docs/supported-configurations); [GCP release notes](https://docs.cloud.google.com/confidential-computing/confidential-vm/docs/release-notes)). Google's AMD story is weaker. **SEV-SNP is documented only on N2D (Milan)**. The newer C3D (Genoa) and C4D (Turin) offer plain SEV, which gives no hardware-signed launch evidence, only a host-managed vTPM ([GCP attestation overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/confidential-computing/confidential-vm/docs/attestation-overview)). The TDX surcharge works out to about $0.042 an hour for 8 vCPU and 32 GB at the published rates ([GCP pricing](https://cloud.google.com/confidential-computing/confidential-vm/pricing)). The notes did not capture base C3 or C4 prices, nor a C4-specific surcharge.

Azure's TDX v6 series went GA on 2026-02-26 on Emerald Rapids, as "Azure confidential compute's first offering to utilize our open-source paravisor, OpenHCL" ([Microsoft GA blog](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/azureconfidentialcomputingblog/announcing-general-availability-of-azure-intel%C2%AE-tdx-confidential-vms/4495693)). The Retail Prices API shows DC8es_v6 at **$0.444/h in East US against $0.403/h for the non-confidential D8s_v6**, about 10% more. The same 10% applies to DC8as_v6 against D8as_v6 ([Azure Retail Prices API](https://prices.azure.com/api/retail/prices)). The comparison VMs are a separate SKU family on separate host pools, so they make a near twin, not an identical one. AWS still has no TDX. Its only hardware-keyed CVM is SEV-SNP on m6a, c6a and r6a. The 10% fee is a separate line item that Savings Plans do not discount. Dedicated Hosts with SNP have worked in every commercial region since July 2026, at no extra charge beyond the host itself ([AWS SEV-SNP guide](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSEC2/latest/UserGuide/sev-snp.html); [AWS What's New](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2026/07/ec2-amd-sev-snp-dedicated-hosts)). AWS has one clean advantage for testing. **m6a.2xlarge is exactly 8 vCPU / 32 GiB, and the same type launched without `AmdSevSnp=enabled` is an identical twin**, even on the same Dedicated Host ([AWS: enable SEV-SNP](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSEC2/latest/UserGuide/snp-work-launch.html)).

Oracle is the quiet surprise. **OCI sells SEV-SNP on Genoa (E5) and Turin (E6) shapes in nearly every public region at no extra cost.** Customers run their own attestation service from Oracle-packaged Veraison components ([OCI Confidential Computing](https://docs.oracle.com/en-us/iaas/Content/Compute/References/confidential_compute.htm); [Oracle Linux remote attestation](https://docs.oracle.com/en-us/iaas/oracle-linux/oci/security-attestation.htm)). IBM sells TDX on Sapphire Rapids bx3dc and cx3dc profiles, verified through standard DCAP, but its regions conflict across its own pages ([IBM VPC docs](https://github.com/ibm-cloud-docs/vpc/blob/master/create-sgx-virtual-server.md); [IBM release notes](https://github.com/ibm-cloud-docs/vpc/blob/master/release-notes.md)). Alibaba's TDX guide, updated 2026-10-28, is technically the closest to the Google experience: configfs-tsm quotes, readable and extendable RTMR0–3, and Ubuntu 24.04 images. It is confined to China, Hong Kong and Singapore zones, and it states that in-OS reboot is unsupported ([Alibaba TDX guide](https://www.alibabacloud.com/help/en/ecs/user-guide/build-a-tdx-confidential-computing-environment)). The notes found no tenant-verifiable CVM at Tencent or Huawei.

Two managed platforms fit the reader's workload. **Tinfoil Containers offers exactly 8 CPUs and 32,768 MB.** Its `cvm_admin: true` profile runs a container Docker-privileged and "may run an inner Docker daemon", so a server can start its own containers ([Tinfoil config docs](https://docs.tinfoil.sh/containers/configuration.md); [cvmimage runtime policy](https://github.com/tinfoilsh/cvmimage/blob/main/docs/runtime-policy.md)). **Phala Cloud runs docker-compose apps in TDX on dstack.** Its own templates mount `/var/run/docker.sock` so that apps can drive sibling containers ([phala-cloud templates](https://github.com/Phala-Network/phala-cloud)). Phala sells tdx.xlarge (8 vCPU / 16 GB) at $0.464/h and tdx.2xlarge (16 vCPU / 32 GB) at $0.928/h. Requests are rounded up to the next shape ([Phala instance types](https://cloud.phala.com/about/instance-types)). SecretVM is cheaper at $0.24/h for 8 vCPU / 16 GB, but it documents neither Docker-socket access nor persistent disks ([SecretAI pricing](https://secretai.scrtlabs.com/pricing)). The Nitro Enclave products (Evervault, Marlin Oyster, OpenSecret) have RAM-only filesystems and vsock-only networking, so they cannot host this workload ([Evervault Enclaves](https://docs.evervault.com/enclaves.md); [AWS Nitro Enclaves](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/enclaves/latest/user/nitro-enclave.html)).

Bare metal with tenant-usable TEEs is scarcer than CPU spec sheets suggest. OpenMetal lists Emerald Rapids servers with "Intel TDX active at base config", but they carry 1–2 TB of RAM ([OpenMetal XXL v4](https://openmetal.io/resources/hardware-details/bare-metal-dedicated-server-xxl-v4-tdx/)). Scaleway documents SEV-SNP on its newer AMD Elastic Metal servers, and tenants cannot change BIOS settings ([Scaleway docs](https://www.scaleway.com/en/docs/elastic-metal/reference-content/elastic-metal-security-features/)). A third-party blog reports SNP on OVHcloud's Turin-based Scale 2026 line, at about USD 423 a month for a high-end US configuration ([bex.co](https://bex.co/blog/2026/07/31/ovhcloud-sev-snp-confidential-computing-bare-metal)). Equinix Metal closed on 30 June 2026 ([Equinix EOS FAQ](https://docs.equinix.com/metal/eos-faq)). For Hetzner, Latitude.sh, Vultr, Leaseweb and Cherry the notes found no evidence either way.

## Only TDX without a paravisor pins a customer's own UKI in hardware evidence

The reader's C3 results set the bar. The raw quote verifies to Intel's root. Google signs an endorsement of the firmware measurement (MRTD). RTMR0 is host-supplied and varies by machine size, so it must be allowlisted per shape. RTMR1 and RTMR2 recompute offline from the UKI, and a host-passed command line appears in RTMR2 only with systemd-stub 257 or later. The other platforms fall into four patterns that differ in exactly where the customer's image stops being covered by vendor-signed evidence.

| Platform | Hardware signer | What the hardware evidence covers | Custom UKI pinned by hardware evidence? | Firmware transparency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GCP C3/C4 TDX | Intel (DCAP) | MRTD, RTMR0 (host config), RTMR1–2 (UKI, command line) | Yes; RTMR1–2 recompute offline, RTMR0 allowlisted per shape | Google-signed endorsement of MRTD |
| Alibaba g8i TDX | Intel (DCAP via Alibaba PCCS) | MRTD, RTMR0–3 readable via configfs-tsm | Probably; custom-image import for TDX undocumented | No published MRTD values found |
| IBM bx3dc TDX | Intel (DCAP) | Standard TD quote | Unknown | Unknown |
| Azure DCesv6 / DCasv6 | Intel or AMD, via the paravisor | Microsoft's paravisor and UEFI only | No; UKI only in vTPM PCRs run by the paravisor | OpenHCL open source but not reproducible; no reference values |
| AWS m6a SNP | AMD (VLEK on shared tenancy) | AWS OVMF at N vCPUs plus initial state | No; UKI only in NitroTPM, signed by AWS | aws/uefi reproducible with Nix; no signed endorsement |
| GCP N2D SNP | AMD (VCEK) | Google OVMF plus initial state | No; kernel only in host-managed vTPM | Signed launch endorsements; firmware images published |
| OCI E5/E6 SNP | AMD | Not stated | Unknown | Reference values shipped in an Oracle RPM |
| Tinfoil Containers | AMD or Intel | Tinfoil OVMF, kernel, initrd; command line carries config hash | Tinfoil's image, not yours; your containers pinned by digest | Byte-for-byte reproducible image; ACPI reconstructed offline |
| Phala Cloud (dstack) | Intel (DCAP) | MRTD, RTMR0–2 (dstack OS), RTMR3 (compose hash, instance, KMS) | dstack's image, not yours; your compose pinned | Reproducible Yocto build; `dstack-mr` recomputes all registers |
| Own bare metal | Intel or AMD (per-chip) | Whatever the customer's VMM measures | Yes, including RTMR0 if the QEMU version is pinned | Customer builds firmware |

### Azure moves the trust root into a Microsoft binary inside the TEE

On Azure, the guest cannot ask the CPU for a fresh report. A paravisor boots first in a higher-privilege layer of the guest (VTL2), owns the vTPM and UEFI, and writes an "HCL report" to vTPM NV index 0x01400001. That report is a raw SNP report or TD report whose report_data hashes a JSON blob of vTPM keys and VM settings ([Azure guest attestation design](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/confidential-computing/guest-attestation-confidential-virtual-machines-design); [OpenHCL architecture](https://openvmm.dev/guide/reference/architecture/openhcl.html)). On TDX, an instance-metadata (IMDS) endpoint turns the TD report into an ordinary Intel-signed quote that DCAP tooling verifies offline, and the Rust crates az-tdx-vtpm and az-snp-vtpm implement the whole chain ([az-tdx-vtpm](https://docs.rs/az-tdx-vtpm/latest/az_tdx_vtpm/)). The catch is coverage. Microsoft's own table says the hardware report covers the paravisor and UEFI, while the guest OS is covered "by the TPM quote". A team running a custom UKI on Azure TDX in production devnets found that **"MRTD and RTMR0-3 cover Microsoft's paravisor"**, that RTMR0–3 read as zeros, and that "OpenHCL builds are not reproducible". It also found that Microsoft publishes "no reference values or signed endorsements", so an allowlist of observed values "would break admissions on every Azure rollout" ([Seismic PR #94](https://github.com/SeismicSystems/seismic-images/pull/94)). An HS3 2026 workshop paper independently says OpenHCL cannot yet be reproduced bit for bit ([HS3 2026](https://hs3-workshop.github.io/2026-papers/hs3-2026-funk-attestation.pdf)).

The practical consequence is subtle. The reader's UKI can still be pinned on Azure, because PCR 4, 9, 11 and 12 follow TCG conventions and recompute offline from the image file, and the vTPM's attestation key is bound into the hardware report. But those PCRs are extended by a Microsoft binary that nobody outside Microsoft can rebuild or check against a published value. That is a step weaker than Google's endorsed firmware, which only sets up the TD and never sits beside the guest. Custom images boot only with Secure Boot off, because the UKI "is not signed by a CA that Azure trusts" (Seismic). The only documented way around Microsoft's attestation and key services, the "NonPersistedTPM" security type, still runs the paravisor. It is described only as part of a "TDX public preview" ([Azure CVM FAQ](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/confidential-computing/confidential-vm-faq)).

### AWS and Google's SNP measure the firmware and stop

On EC2 the guest gets a raw AMD report from `/dev/sev-guest` with no AWS service in the path. That is good for offline verification, but the launch measurement is "a cryptographic hash of the initial boot state", meaning AWS's OVMF at a given vCPU count ([AWS SNP attestation](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSEC2/latest/UserGuide/snp-attestation.html)). AWS does make that firmware reproducible. `nix-build --pure` on aws/uefi reproduces the binary, and `sev-snp-measure --vmm-type=ec2` predicts the digest ([github.com/aws/uefi](https://github.com/aws/uefi)). Nothing past the firmware enters AMD-signed evidence. A 2025 systematization paper concluded that on AWS "only the virtual firmware, but not the kernel, is measured" and that "AWS becomes a trusted stakeholder on attestation" ([SoK, arXiv 2503.08256](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.08256)). An open-source project that captured EC2 reports in October 2026 summarised the result as **"no boot pin"** and "no machine identity on EC2" ([vault-genome PR #210](https://github.com/vault-genome/vaultgenome-core/pull/210)). The kernel can be pinned only through AWS's Attestable AMIs. Those use NitroTPM PCR4 (the UKI) and PCR12 (the command line), precomputed with `nitro-tpm-pcr-compute --image UKI.efi`, with dm-verity or erofs recommended for the root ([AWS Attestable AMIs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSEC2/latest/UserGuide/attestable-ami.html)). But the NitroTPM document is "signed by the Nitro Hypervisor" under AWS's own PKI, outside the SNP boundary ([AWS NitroTPM document validation](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSEC2/latest/UserGuide/nitrotpm-attestation-document-validate.html)). On AWS, then, AMD vouches that memory is encrypted under AWS's firmware, and AWS alone vouches for what booted. The reader's UKI-plus-dm-verity artifact maps neatly onto the Attestable AMI model, which makes AWS easy to try and impossible to make equivalent.

Google's SNP on N2D has the same shape with better firmware transparency. The report covers the launch measurement only, and Google publishes signed launch endorsements and the production firmware images, verifiable with gce-tcb-verifier ([google/gce-tcb-verifier](https://github.com/google/gce-tcb-verifier)). The kernel, command line and root filesystem can be attested only through the Shielded VM vTPM, which Google's own docs describe as host-managed ([GCP attestation overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/confidential-computing/confidential-vm/docs/attestation-overview)). An in-TEE vTPM would close this gap on AMD. That is the SVSM, a small service module running at the guest's most privileged level. It appears only in Google community posts, not in a product. COCONUT-SVSM made its vTPM state persistent only in a development release on 28 August 2026 ([Google Cloud community blog](https://security.googlecloudcommunity.com/community-blog-42/beyond-confidential-establishing-trust-in-your-computing-environment-6290); [COCONUT-SVSM v2026.08-devel](https://ratatoskr.run/linux-coco/2026/08/17474893)). OCI's documentation describes Veraison reference values shipped as CoRIM files in an RPM, but never says what the launch measurement covers ([Oracle Linux attestation](https://docs.oracle.com/en-us/iaas/oracle-linux/oci/security-attestation.htm)). Whether a customer kernel can be pinned there is unknown.

### Managed platforms measure their own image and your container digests

Tinfoil and dstack invert the model. The customer supplies containers, not a boot image. In Tinfoil's case the CPU measures OVMF, kernel and initrd, and "the kernel command line embeds the SHA-256 hash of `tinfoil-config.yml`". The config must live in a public GitHub repository, each release tag is logged to Sigstore's Rekor, the CVM image is "byte-for-byte reproducible", and client SDKs verify the chain to the AMD or Intel root ([Tinfoil attestation architecture](https://docs.tinfoil.sh/verification/attestation-architecture)). Tinfoil's `hardware-measurements` repository goes one step beyond Google. It "reproducibly generates the TDX platform measurements", with "ACPI tables reconstructed offline" on QEMU 10.1.0 ([tinfoilsh/hardware-measurements](https://github.com/tinfoilsh/hardware-measurements)). **That is the RTMR0 recomputation that is impossible on Google**, where the host supplies RTMR0. dstack makes the same move with `dstack-mr`, which recomputes MRTD and RTMR0–2 from the Yocto-built OS image and VM configuration. RTMR3 records the compose hash, instance ID and key provider ([Phala: verify the platform](https://docs.phala.com/phala-cloud/attestation/verify-the-platform.md)). An open dstack pull request (#597) adds UKI disk boot and reports MRTD, RTMR1 and RTMR2 matching on real hardware ([dstack attestation notes](https://vat.pages.dev/Dstack-TEE/dstack/blob/master/attestation.md)). That would let a self-hosted dstack carry the reader's own UKI, but managed Phala Cloud boots Phala's OS.

Both platforms share one gap that matters for this workload. **Containers that the server starts at run time are outside the measurement.** On Tinfoil only the admin image's digest is pinned, and "guest network rules are not a security boundary against the CVM administrator" ([cvmimage runtime policy](https://github.com/tinfoilsh/cvmimage/blob/main/docs/runtime-policy.md)). On Phala only the compose hash is pinned. The reader's server would have to carry its own digest allowlist inside the measured image, or extend RTMR3 for each container it starts. The notes did not confirm that dstack exposes an extend call for that.

### Rented bare metal makes everything recomputable and nothing locatable

Owning the hypervisor makes every register reproducible from source. KVM gained TDX host support in Linux 6.16, QEMU 10.1 supports TDX and launches TDX or SNP guests from IGVM files, and Ubuntu 25.10 ships TDX host support without a PPA ([Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/news/Intel-TDX-Host-KVM-Linux-6.16); [Phoronix QEMU 10.1](https://www.phoronix.com/news/QEMU-10.1-Released); [canonical/tdx](https://github.com/canonical/tdx)). On SNP, measured direct boot puts kernel, initrd and command-line hashes into the launch digest. That needs exactly the VMM and firmware control that EC2 and Google withhold ([SNPGuard, arXiv 2406.01186](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.01186)). The price is operational. A TDX host needs Intel PCS registration through the multi-package registration agent, a local PCCS and a quote-generation service. And since the operator runs the hypervisor, the "operator cannot read it" claim rests entirely on the customer verifying the operator's own host stack.

## Proof of location now decides whether an attestation means anything

### The interposer attacks turned provenance from a nicety into a requirement

The physical attacks of 2024–2026 do not merely leak memory. They break attestation itself. **TEE.fail (October 2025) used a passive DDR5 interposer under $1,000 to recover Intel's Provisioning Certification Key and forge TDX quotes that pass DCAP at "UpToDate".** It also extracted keys from an SEV-SNP VM with ciphertext hiding on, and Intel and AMD both declared it out of scope ([tee.fail](https://tee.fail/)). **DDRop (September 2026, ACM CCS 2026) built an active DDR5 interposer from about $159 of parts.** It drops memory writes so the CPU reads stale but validly encrypted data. On TDX it read victim memory and flipped a TD into debug mode, both under the "logical integrity" mode. It also overwrote a launch measurement so that an attacker's VM passed attestation, which the authors argue would work under cryptographic integrity too but could not test. Intel issued no CVE, and AMD called it out of scope in AMD-SB-3048 ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/09/new-ddrop-attack-breaks-intel-tdx-and.html)). On DDR4 hardware, which includes AWS's and Google's Milan SNP hosts, Battering RAM ($47.62 of parts) replays an SNP launch digest so a modified VM attests as genuine on fully patched systems ([batteringram.eu](https://batteringram.eu/)). BadRAM did the same through a $10 SPD rewrite until AMD's firmware fix ([SecurityWeek](https://www.securityweek.com/badram-attack-uses-10-equipment-to-break-amd-processor-protections/)). A stolen PCK chains to Intel's root and carries a genuine platform ID, so TCB status cannot catch it. The only defence is knowing **which machine** produced a quote and trusting whoever guards it.

On that axis the providers separate sharply. Google is the only one with a public, checkable answer: the reader's quotes name the instance, and Google publishes a registry of its TDX hosts that Flashbots' verifier already uses ([Flashbots forum](https://collective.flashbots.net/t/flashbots-attestation-verification-now-supports-google-cloud-provenance-checks/5954)). AWS's shared-tenancy reports are signed by a VLEK, an endorsement key that AMD issues only to an enrolled cloud. That proves "an AMD chip provisioned for AWS" but leaves out CHIP_ID and binds no account, instance or region ([AMD SEV-SNP primer, arXiv 2608.04039](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.04039)). One project claims the VLEK certificate carries a per-region provider ID such as `cc-us-east-2.amazonaws.com` (vault-genome PR #210). That would give region-level provenance, but nobody has independently confirmed it. Azure ties evidence to its fleet only through an attestation-key certificate "signed by Azure CA" and a `vmUniqueId` in the hashed runtime claims. The notes found no Azure host registry. Oracle, IBM and Alibaba document nothing beyond the CPU vendor's chain.

The managed platforms are weakest here, and they say so. Tinfoil trusts "the silicon vendor" and states that "an attacker with physical access to the hardware can potentially compromise the enclave". It documents no data-centre locations ([Tinfoil confidentiality](https://docs.tinfoil.sh/cc/confidentiality)). Phala runs on "OVH and bare-metal servers in professionally managed Tier-3+ data centers" ([Phala on TEE.fail](https://phala.com/posts/phala-ddr5)). A May 2026 survey added that it also uses OpenMetal, that dstack was among the systems TEE.fail attacked, and that **"a TDX attestation from an OVH server is indistinguishable from one produced in an attacker's garage"**. The same survey found that only Level 1 (human witnesses) of the Proof of Cloud registry is operational, with no queryable interface ([ethresear.ch survey](https://ethresear.ch/t/physical-integrity-attestation-and-the-state-of-permissionless-tees/24964)). dstack can at least allowlist devices: its on-chain `allowedDeviceIds` derive from the TDX platform ID ([Phala on-chain KMS](https://docs.phala.com/phala-cloud/key-management/understanding-onchain-kms.md)). That pins the reader's evidence to known chips, though not to known rooms. Self-run bare metal has no provenance service at all beyond the Proof of Cloud alliance, whose public page names no registered facilities ([proofofcloud.org](https://proofofcloud.org/)).

### Milan hosts carry the heaviest software-attack record

Host-software attacks need no physical access, and AMD's older parts have taken the most. RMPocalypse (CVE-2025-0033) let "a single 8-byte write" defeat SNP on Zen 3–5 until firmware fixes shipped ([SecurityWeek](https://www.securityweek.com/rmpocalypse-new-attack-breaks-amd-confidential-computing/)). Heracles uses page relocation as a plaintext oracle on Milan and Genoa. It is stopped only by Turin's ciphertext hiding or the PAGE_SWAP_DISABLE guest policy added in SNP ABI 1.58 ([Heracles](https://heracles-attack.github.io); [AMD-SB-3021](https://www.amd.com/en/resources/product-security/bulletin/amd-sb-3021.html)). StackWarp abuses SMT siblings on Zen 1–5 ([USENIX Security 2026](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity26/presentation/zhang-ruiyi)). Fabricked lets a malicious UEFI leave the RMP uninitialised and forge reports ([Tom's Hardware](https://tomshardware.com/pc-components/researchers-attack-amds-infinity-fabric-to-bypass-hardware-security-protections-with-fabricked-flaw-lets-malicious-cloud-hosts-silently-read-confidential-vm-memory-and-forge-attestation-reports)). Most serious for AWS, a May 2026 paper extracted the Milan root seed by software alone and forged VCEK-signed reports "for any firmware version" ([arXiv 2605.12990](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12990)). Whether that extends to AWS's VLEK-signed reports is not established in the notes. But the fleet AWS exposes, and Google's N2D, is the generation with the most published breaks. OCI's Turin E6 shapes are the only rented SNP with ciphertext hiding. TDX's record is lighter: TDXdown was fixed in the TDX module, Heckler in the guest kernel, and CVE-2025-30513 surfaced in a joint Google–Intel audit ([SecurityWeek](https://www.securityweek.com/new-counterseveillance-and-tdxdown-attacks-target-amd-and-intel-tees/); [TechTimes](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/328955/20261014/intel-ends-sgx-era-diamond-rapids-pivots-confidential-computing-fully-tdx.htm)). But TDXRay recovers LLM prompts from host-observable access traces without breaking any encryption ([TDXRay](https://tdxray.cpusec.org/)).

### Who has to be trusted on each platform

Putting these together, every option trusts the CPU vendor's root and someone's physical custody. Beyond that the lists diverge. On Google TDX the customer additionally trusts Google's endorsed firmware, its host-supplied RTMR0 inputs (ACPI tables, TD HOB), its host registry, and its data-centre security. Azure adds a non-reproducible paravisor that runs inside the TEE and is updated by Azure rollouts. AWS adds the Nitro hypervisor and AWS's PKI for everything past firmware, and the customer is limited to Milan. OCI adds Oracle's packaged reference values and an undocumented measurement scope. Tinfoil adds Tinfoil's build pipeline, GitHub's signing identity and an unknown facility. The customer's admin container gains full control of the CVM. Phala adds a KMS that Phala runs in a TEE, whoever owns the on-chain app contract (by default the deploying wallet), and OVH-class custody. dstack's verifier also had a 2026 critical bug in which dcap-qvl skipped Quoting Enclave identity checks, fixed in 0.3.9 ([Phala security update](https://phala.com/posts/dstack-security-update-attestation-pipeline-hardening)). Self-run bare metal removes the cloud's firmware from the trust list but makes the operator, the very party the customer distrusts, both hypervisor and custodian.

## Overheads are small; operational limits and prices differ more

### Performance

The independent SIGMETRICS 2025 study, on Emerald Rapids TDX and Genoa SNP, measured steady-state overheads of **4.1% for TDX and 7.3% for SNP**. VM-exiting instructions cost 472% and 240% more. Bounce-buffered networking under saturation cost far more: HTTPS throughput fell 31% on TDX and 10% on SNP, recovering to 10% and 4% with guest idle polling. Storage that was not CPU-bound showed almost no penalty ([Misono et al.](https://dse.in.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/sigmetrics25summer-CVM-Explained.pdf)). Phoronix found 2–10% for SNP on Turin, more for I/O-heavy work ([Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/review/amd-epyc-9005-sev-snp)). The reader's C3 results fit this picture exactly. Lightly loaded request latency showed no change. dm-crypt with its work queues bypassed cost about 2%. The default dm-crypt path lost about 20% of fsync commits, which is consistent with extra thread wakeups and HLT exits per I/O, though no paper has measured dm-crypt inside a CVM. Two cautions apply to cross-provider comparisons. Google warned that SNP VMs "might have longer boot times and performance changes" from August to November 2026 because of a guest-kernel migration ([GCP release notes](https://docs.cloud.google.com/confidential-computing/confidential-vm/docs/release-notes)). And the reader's own finding that **disk speed varied more than twofold between individual C3 VMs for reasons unrelated to TDX** means a two-day test with one VM per arm cannot separate a 5% TDX effect from host noise. Each arm needs several VMs, or one twin on the same host, which only AWS Dedicated Hosts offer.

Azure advertises up to 205k IOPS and 4 GB/s remote storage on its TDX v6 VMs, plus local NVMe on the "d" variants, but publishes no confidential-versus-plain overhead figure ([Microsoft GA blog](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/azureconfidentialcomputingblog/announcing-general-availability-of-azure-intel%C2%AE-tdx-confidential-vms/4495693)). Its FAQ, updated October 2026, still says Accelerated Networking cannot be enabled on any confidential VM ([Azure CVM FAQ](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/confidential-computing/confidential-vm-faq)). AWS publishes no SNP I/O figures at all. Alibaba warns that "performance may be lower" and that bounce-buffer (SWIOTLB) limits can crash VMs with several network interfaces or heavy I/O ([Alibaba TDX guide](https://www.alibabacloud.com/help/en/ecs/user-guide/build-a-tdx-confidential-computing-environment)).

### Limits

No rented CVM offers nested virtualization. That rules out microVMs inside the customer VM everywhere, and it is why the reader's server must run Docker containers rather than VMs. Azure states it outright. AWS's February 2026 nested virtualization covers only Intel C8i/M8i/R8i, never SNP types ([Azure CVM FAQ](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/confidential-computing/confidential-vm-faq); [AWS What's New](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2026/02/amazon-ec2-nested-virtualization-on-virtual)). TDX partitioning and SVSM-on-TDX remain prototypes ([coconut-svsm #815](https://github.com/coconut-svsm/svsm/issues/815)). No platform live-migrates a TDX or SNP VM in production. Google requires `onHostMaintenance=TERMINATE` and gives C4 TDX seven days' notice. AWS gives 14 days and expects the customer to stop and start the instance. Azure has only demonstrated confidential live migration, at Build 2026 ([GCP supported configurations](https://docs.cloud.google.com/confidential-computing/confidential-vm/docs/supported-configurations); [AWS SEV-SNP guide](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSEC2/latest/UserGuide/sev-snp.html); [Azure sovereignty blog](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/azureconfidentialcomputingblog/azure-confidential-computing-for-digital-sovereignty-and-regulated-workloads/4529932)). An always-on product must therefore plan for scheduled restarts and for disk keys that can be released again after each boot.

Other limits are platform-specific. On AWS, SNP cannot be combined with Nitro Enclaves or hibernation, and firmware on Dedicated Hosts changes only when the host is re-allocated. On Azure, confidential OS-disk encryption is fixed at creation, binds keys to the vTPM through Microsoft's attestation service, and "only supports disks that are smaller than 128 GB". A design that wants the operator out of the key path should use `VMGuestStateOnly` and encrypt inside the guest ([Azure CVM overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/confidential-computing/confidential-vm-overview)). OCI's confidential shapes exclude preemptible capacity and capacity reservations. Tinfoil keeps Docker's writable layers, images and volumes in RAM, so 32 GB must also hold image layers. Its encrypted persistent volumes are beta, and new orgs start at five containers and 32 GB of storage ([Tinfoil limits](https://docs.tinfoil.sh/containers/limits.md); [Tinfoil overview](https://docs.tinfoil.sh/containers/overview)). Capacity data are thin everywhere. The reader found non-confidential C3 scarce. Azure warns that "quotas are credit limits, not capacity guarantees" and that confidential families usually need a quota request. The notes found no capacity reports for any other provider.

### Price

For one always-on customer VM, the list prices cluster near $280–$330 a month on the hyperscalers that publish them: AWS m6a.2xlarge with SNP at about $277, Azure DC8as_v6 at $292 and DC8es_v6 at $324. Google's TDX surcharge alone is about $31 a month on top of a base price the notes did not capture, and Oracle charges nothing extra. Phala's nearest shape costs $677 a month because 32 GB forces 16 vCPUs. Tinfoil's last seen figure, $0.28/h for 4 CPU / 16 GB plus a $1,000 monthly platform fee, would come to about $1,400 a month for one customer. The platform fee would spread across customers, but the figure came from a search snapshot and the live page shows no prices ([tinfoil.sh/containers](https://tinfoil.sh/containers)). Bare metal is cheapest per customer only at density. A roughly $423-a-month Turin server would host many 8/32 guests, but it is billed monthly and needs its own attestation infrastructure.

## Test GCP C4 and Azure TDX first, then the managed platforms

The ranking weighs three things: how close each candidate can come to the C3 baseline on pinning and provenance, whether it could become a second production venue or a second vendor for key splitting, and what a two-day test can actually settle.

### 1. GCP C4 TDX: the cheapest way to widen the baseline

C4 matters because it is the same trust model on a newer CPU (Granite Rapids, TDX module 2.0) in 15 zones. It could relieve the C3 capacity squeeze the reader hit. The test needs the existing project and tooling and a c4-standard-8 confidential VM next to a non-confidential c4-standard-8. Re-run the C3 suite: quote verification, endorsement check, RTMR1/2 recomputation, systemd-stub 257 command-line injection, the host-registry lookup, dm-crypt fsync and latency. Cost is the C4 base price plus about $2 of TDX surcharge for 48 hours. The notes captured neither the base price nor a C4-specific surcharge, so check before launching. The test can show whether RTMR0 and the endorsement format differ on C4, whether C4 hosts appear in the same registry, and whether Hyperdisk behaves differently under TDX. It cannot show capacity at scale, and per-VM disk variance means it needs at least three VMs per arm to say anything about I/O.

### 2. Azure DC8es_v6: the only other hyperscaler TDX

Azure is the only plausible second hyperscaler for TDX. The test exists to decide whether the paravisor model is acceptable. It needs a pay-as-you-go subscription (free accounts lack the cores), a quota request for the DCESv6 family in eastus or westus3, the Gen 2 VHD and Compute Gallery path with `SecurityType=ConfidentialVMSupported`, `--security-type ConfidentialVM --os-disk-security-encryption-type VMGuestStateOnly`, and Secure Boot off ([Azure custom image guide](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/confidential-computing/how-to-create-custom-image-confidential-vm)). Tooling: tpm2-tools for the HCL report and quotes, az-tdx-vtpm for the IMDS TD quote, dcap-qvl or go-tdx-guest for offline verification, and the TCG event log. Two days of DC8es_v6 plus a D8s_v6 twin cost about **$41**, and about $60 with a DC8as_v6 SNP arm added. The test can show whether the reader's UKI boots, whether PCR 4/9/11/12 match offline recomputation, how the attestation key binds to the hardware report, whether MRTD differs between two VMs, and whether configfs-tsm works under OpenHCL. The notes expect it not to. The test cannot show that the paravisor is trustworthy, and it cannot observe how often MRTD changes across Azure rollouts. That needs weeks of sampling.

### 3. Tinfoil Containers: the best managed fit, if access is granted

Tinfoil is worth testing as a product path rather than a like-for-like VM. It is the only managed offering with the exact shape, an inner Docker daemon and a Sigstore-anchored release log. The test needs an org account (the page says "Preview Access", so contact sales), a public config repo, and a container image pinned by digest. The flow is `measure-image-action`, then `tinfoil container create`, then `tinfoil attestation verify` from the laptop ([tinfoil-persistent-storage-example](https://github.com/tinfoilsh/tinfoil-persistent-storage-example)). Compute would be about $27 for two days if the snapshot price scales linearly. The real cost may be a platform-fee commitment. The test can show whether the reader's server runs under `cvm_admin` with an inner dockerd, how the beta encrypted volume behaves across restarts, whether a customer-run keyserver can gate secrets ([tinfoilsh/keyserver](https://github.com/tinfoilsh/keyserver)), and whether the platform-measurement repo really recomputes RTMR0 offline. It cannot show where the hardware is. It does not boot the reader's UKI, so the results are not comparable register by register with C3.

### 4. Phala Cloud: the cheapest managed test, with an on-chain upgrade log

Phala needs only a card and the `phala` CLI. Deploy a compose file whose app mounts the Docker socket, fetch `/attestation`, then run `dstack-verifier` and `dstack-mr` locally against the meta-dstack release. Read `allowedComposeHashes` and `allowedDeviceIds` on the app's Base contract ([Phala: verify the platform](https://docs.phala.com/phala-cloud/attestation/verify-the-platform.md)). Two days on tdx.2xlarge with 100 GB of disk cost about **$45**. The test can show full offline recomputation of MRTD and RTMR0–3, whether `allowedDeviceIds` can pin a known set of chips, and the effect of mounting docker.sock on what the end customer verifies. It cannot show physical custody beyond "OVH or OpenMetal". It does not test the reader's UKI either. Running self-hosted dstack-cloud with `platform: gcp` would combine dstack's tooling with Google's provenance ([dstack-cloud configuration](https://docs.phala.com/dstack-cloud/configuration.md)), but the notes did not examine that path.

### 5. AWS m6a.2xlarge SNP: a second CPU vendor with an exact twin

AWS earns a place as the cheapest AMD arm with a perfect twin and a documented raw-report path, which makes it the obvious second vendor for any cross-vendor key split. The test needs a normal account in us-east-2, a UEFI AMI, `--cpu-options AmdSevSnp=enabled`, snpguest in the guest, AMD's VLEK chain, and aws/uefi with `sev-snp-measure --vmm-type=ec2 --vcpus=8` on the laptop. For the UKI, register the image with NitroTPM enabled and compare PCR4/PCR12 with `nitro-tpm-pcr-compute` ([AWS SNP attestation](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSEC2/latest/UserGuide/snp-attestation.html); [AWS PCR computation](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSEC2/latest/UserGuide/create-pcr-compute.html)). Two days of SNP plus twin cost under **$40**. The test can show the policy bits AWS sets (debug, SMT, migration agent), the REPORTED_TCB against the RMPocalypse and StackWarp fixes, whether the VLEK certificate really carries a regional provider ID, whether NitroTPM and SNP coexist on one instance, and a clean overhead measurement. It cannot make AWS's kernel evidence AMD-rooted. No test on managed EC2 can do that.

### 6. OCI E6.Flex SNP: modern AMD at no premium, measurement scope unknown

OCI is the only rented SNP on Turin with ciphertext hiding, and it charges nothing extra. Its documentation leaves the decisive question unanswered: what the launch measurement covers, and whether a custom kernel can enter it. The test needs an OCI account, an E6.Flex instance of 4 OCPUs and 32 GB with memory encryption enabled at launch, Oracle Linux 9 with UEK 8 or Ubuntu 24.04, and the Veraison packages. In parallel, run snpguest directly to compare the raw report with Oracle's CoRIM reference values. Cost was not captured, but there is no premium. The test can settle whether a custom image imports with SNP, whether reports are VCEK- or VLEK-signed, and whether the digest varies by shape. It is likely to land in the same "firmware only" category as AWS and Google SNP.

### 7. Rented bare metal: a reference build, not a candidate

A bare-metal build is the only way to see every register recomputed from source, including RTMR0. It is therefore the best calibration for what the clouds hide, and the worst answer to "is the chip where it claims to be". Candidates are OVHcloud's Scale 2026 for SNP (about $423 a month in the US, per a secondary source) or an OpenMetal TDX server (price not captured, 1–2 TB of RAM). The build needs Ubuntu 25.10 or the Canonical TDX PPA, QEMU 10.1, Intel PCS registration for TDX, and dstack-mr or sev-snp-measure. Monthly billing makes a two-day test cost a month. Testing IBM and Alibaba is not recommended now. IBM's documentation leaves image import and firmware values unknown. Alibaba's TDX is technically close to Google's but confined to Asian regions, and it disallows in-guest reboot.

### What only hands-on tests can settle

Some questions recur across providers, and no amount of reading will answer them. On Azure: whether OpenHCL's MRTD is stable across VMs and how often rollouts change it; whether the UKI's PCR 12 carries host-passed command lines the way RTMR2 does on Google; and whether NonPersistedTPM works on GA DCesv6. On AWS: whether NitroTPM and SNP can run together; whether the VLEK subject names a region; and what the guest policy allows. On GCP C4: whether RTMR0, the endorsement and the registry behave as on C3. On Tinfoil: how its persistent-volume key is derived, and whether the customer, Tinfoil or a keyserver releases it. On Phala: whether run-time containers can be extended into RTMR3. On every platform: whether the default memory-integrity mode is logical or cryptographic, which decides part of DDRop's reach; disk I/O variance between individual VMs, measured with enough VMs per arm; and real-world capacity for always-on fleets.

## Where the notes disagree or run thin

Several facts conflict across sources. Azure's GA blog lists South Central US as a TDX region, but the price API shows no DC8es_v6 price there. Third-party aggregators list DC8es_v6 at $0.403/h, which looks like a pre-September price. Azure's FAQ still says Accelerated Networking is unavailable on confidential VMs while the v6 launch advertises 40 Gbps through Azure Boost. IBM's pages disagree on its TDX regions. Intel's white paper calls cryptographic memory integrity the default, while DDRop's coverage says the default is logical integrity ([Intel TDX white paper](https://kib.kiev.ua/x86docs/Intel/WhitePapers/869103-0010.pdf); [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/09/new-ddrop-attack-breaks-intel-tdx-and.html)). Tinfoil's quotas page currently says five containers for Tier 1, but an older snippet said ten. Its price is known only from a snapshot. Phala's older pricing page lists a different tdx.2xlarge shape, and its chain-of-trust page lists three RTMR3 events where the attestation-fields page lists four. Earlier research for this project described Tinfoil's volume key as operator-held. The current notes say only that its derivation is undocumented, so treat it as unknown. Tinfoil's own docs say encrypted volumes are not protected against replay of older sectors ([Tinfoil attestation architecture](https://docs.tinfoil.sh/verification/attestation-architecture)).

The thin areas matter as much. No source gave base prices for GCP C3, C4 or N2D, nor OCI, IBM or Alibaba prices. Nothing documented OCI's measurement scope, IBM's custom-image or firmware story, or Alibaba's custom-image import for TDX. Nothing confirmed Google's SNP zones or any SNP on C3D/C4D. No independent benchmarks covered Azure or AWS confidential VMs, Granite Rapids TDX, or dm-crypt inside any CVM. Capacity data exist only from the reader's own C3 experience. Several key AWS facts (no boot pin, CHIP_ID zero, a per-region provider ID) come from a single open-source project's October 2026 record that nobody has verified. The OVHcloud SNP and TDX claims rest on press and a third-party blog, and the DDRop and TEE.fail findings were read through project pages and coverage, not the papers.

## Conclusion

The field has sorted itself by who owns the layer between the CPU and the customer's kernel. Where nobody does (Google TDX, Alibaba TDX, bare metal), hardware evidence reaches the customer's own image. Where the cloud runs a paravisor (Azure) or a hypervisor-hosted TPM (AWS, Google SNP), evidence stops at the cloud's code. Where a platform owns the whole image (Tinfoil, dstack), evidence reaches the customer's containers but not their OS. No offering combines all three things the reader wants: a pinned custom image, recomputable firmware including RTMR0, and named, checkable hardware custody. Google comes closest on the first and third, Tinfoil and dstack on the second. A practical design may borrow across that divide, for example by reconstructing Google's RTMR0 offline the way Tinfoil's hardware-measurements repository does for its own QEMU hosts.

Since attestation now fails against anyone with brief physical access, the choice of venue is less about CPU features than about whose custody the customer is asked to believe and how they can check it. That favours staying on Google TDX as the primary venue, testing C4 for capacity, and using Azure TDX or AWS SNP as a second, independently guarded vendor rather than as an equivalent replacement. Their weaker image pinning is then contained by threshold key release, so that no single venue's software or physical compromise exposes a customer's disk.
